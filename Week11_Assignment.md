When it comes to protecting animal welfare, I am not the person I want to be, and by that, I mean that I am not a vegan. It feels like a moral failing to be so aware of the inhumane conditions animals in factory farms endure and yet, fuel the dairy and meat markets with my own consumption. I feel a lot of guilt about not adhering to veganism.

Earlier in the summer, Billie Eilish drew criticism after saying that someone could not both eat meat and love animals. Her statement and my guilt share the same underlying logic: by feeding money into a market that is founded on the violent (and often, grotesquely so) exploitation of animals, we are participants in that brutality. 

However, that logic misses the mark a bit. Firstly, many non-vegans have different reasons for their decision, including a lack of a decision. In areas such as food desserts and food swamps, there may be an absence of nutritional vegan food or ethically sourced meat and dairy products. Such options also tend to be more expensive, rendering them less accessible to people who don’t have the wealth that people, like Billie Eilish, do. 

I’m not trying to condemn veganism or the people who (sometimes misguidedly) promote veganism. I do believe that in an ideal world, people would consume, not necessarily no meat, but significantly less meat. I do believe that in an ideal world, the United States’ “beef culture,” that is the term I will use for referring to our cultural emphasis on consuming meat, specifically beef, would be obsolete, recognized for its development through the patriarchal branding of it as some way to assert masculinity through subjugating and consuming other beings (see The Sexual Politics of Meat). And I do believe that in an ideal world, all meat and dairy products would be derived from animals, that have been raised in humane conditions by people, who actively sought to minimize their suffering. 

However, this is not an ideal world, and it is essential to note that veganism is unsustainable for a lot of people through various channels of inaccessibility. To directly address Billie Eilish’s statement, this lack of accessibility does not make people incapable of loving animals. I believe it is a false dilemma to insinuate people can either love animals or eat meat, because a love for animals is not inherently centered on veganism, but rather the intention to recognize animals beyond what they’ve been objectified by humans as (food, labor, companionship, etc). Omnivorous people can, and a good amount of them likely do, oppose threats to animal welfare, such as habitat destruction, extinctions, and unnecessarily painful and excessive animal slaughter and exploitation, because they can recognize animals as existing beyond our assigned purposes for them. 

Speciesism is the differential treatment of sentient beings based on their species membership. In the current culture of the United States, we can see how humans have fabricated hierarchies of species, based in human-centered standards of intelligence, physical ability, etc., that, in short, prioritize humans before all else. As with most hierarchies, the interests of oppressors are prioritized before the welfare of the oppressed, enabling the objectification of the low ranks to how they can meet the higher ranks’ wants and needs (in the case of animals, we see them reduced to companionship, cloth material, food, experiment subjects, entertainment, etc.), justifying the exploitation of the oppressed. 

In other words, a tale as old as time is the invention that some are inferior to others, so that the suffering of those some for the thriving of those others can be rationalized. Within the prison-industrial-complex, Black and Brown people are often objectified as inexpensive labor to be turned for profit. Within the patriarchal vision of the nuclear family, women are objectified as domestic labor, as well as the accessory to the male identity, that can prove his most respectable scope of domination by existing within it. 

Of course, I would now want to point out that the institutionalized exploitation of oppressed humans are entirely different to that of animals, because while there are fundamental differences among species, there are none within the human species. While the common thread of fabricated hierarchy, created with economic incentive, lies within both, neither can be viewed as synonymous to one another, as the way humans endure oppression in practice is far different than the ways animals do. I am not trying to equate the two, but rather note the common theme of how social constructs justify violent exploitation. 

While researching for this article, I came across the animal-industrial complex, a concept I haven’t heard before. Essentially, the animal-industrial complex describes the institutionalized exploitation of animals, argued to include all economic activity involving animals, from blood sports to apiculture. Killing billions of land and aquatic animals every year, the animal-industrial complex has some responsibility for climate change, ocean acidification, biodiversity loss, and the spreading of diseases from animals to humans.

That is always heavy on my mind, and despite understanding barriers to veganism, I do feel guilty for not being entirely vegan, and, on a greater scale, benefiting just through my identity as a human from systems of speciesism. To return to my initial point, when it comes to protecting animal welfare, I am not the person I want to be. I have a lot of conflicting emotions about my consumption of meat and dairy products, and I’m definitely still exploring and making sense of them. 

Mostly, I try to remain curious. When I find out someone I’m talking to is vegan, I make an effort to ask them about what that looks like for them: what motivated them to pursue veganism; how long they’ve been vegan; if they find it difficult to access tasty and healthy food; what their thoughts are on impossible meat; if they have any particular favorite vegan recipes; etc. Similarly, when I come across people who work in the dairy and/or meat industries, I try to ask them about what that looks like for them: what led them into those industries; how animal welfare regulation may or may not affect their operations; what they would like to see changed. As I make up my mind, it has helped me to smooth out some of the ambiguity by listening to and assessing others’ perspectives and experiences. 

For people who do have the privilege of being able to access more ethically sourced meat and dairy products, the ASPCA’s ShopKind website has also been pretty helpful when I’m writing out grocery lists, so I’d definitely recommend taking a look at that. (https://www.aspca.org/shopkind) Otherwise, I’ll just sometimes keep an eye out for the Certified Humane label and, when deciding between similar products with and without the label, I’ll try to select the ones with. 

Although I’m a more conscious consumer than before, I still do consume some ambiguously sourced meat and dairy products, and even as I write this, I feel bad about that. I don’t really have a way to mend that invented hierarchy creates exploitation and that I am somehow justified as a beneficiary of that exploitation: an exploitation that feels essential to the welfare of omnivorous humans, who make up a large majority of us, whether by an autonomous decision or not. 

For now, I want to acknowledge that loving animals may not require never eating a burger or killing an insect, but it does require the understanding that we are not superior to other animals and that we are not entitled to their suffering. The unjust and unnecessary cruelty and brutality onto animals, that are either defenseless to begin with or rendered defenseless through the mutilation of their horns, claws, or teeth, is one of the many pains humans have inflicted upon the natural environment. The necessary first step to stopping this is to, as a collective, deconstruct the hierarchies that justify the violent exploitation of the animals that walk the earth with us. 

If such a cultural shift occurs, small steps pave paths for more to come: as more consume ethically-sourced food, clothing, entertainment, etc., companies’ costs will be spread over a larger mass of consumers, enabling the lowering of prices, which may make such goods and services more accessible. 

When creatures are at our mercy, we are their keepers.
